# Animation rules: anatomy, lighting, visuals (from the creator, verbatim)

**NOTE: READ FIRST** Do not change any of the words based on these suggestions. This list is only for anatomical and visual looks.
- Keep the script exactly as written, word for word: dialogue, narration, subtitles, and all on-screen text.
- Nothing in this list is a reason to add, remove, reword, shorten, or reorder any line, scene, or story event.
- Nothing in this list is a reason to change voices, music, or sound effects.
- These rules only change how things look: anatomy, poses, movement, lighting, color, and visual detail.
- If a rule ever seems to conflict with the script or its directions, the script wins. Apply the rule only where it fits.

**General**
- Apply every rule to every character in every shot, background characters included, not just where a problem was first spotted.
- Review the whole animation in separate passes, one per section below. Check stills at every keyframe, expression change, and cut.
- Every line or shape on a body must match real anatomy or a real clothing fold. Delete stray lines (e.g., a line jutting off the neck) and leftover blobs that read as "mud."

**Hands & grips**
- Hands read as hands: palm, separate fingers, thumb in the correct position. Never a blob.
- No outlines inside the hand that break its structure, and no small outlined ovals. Interior details blend into the surrounding skin tone at their edges.
- Held objects sit in the palm with the fingers wrapped around them. Raise the hand to a natural holding height.
- Held objects hide whatever part of the hand is behind them. On a flat object (letter, parchment), show the thumb on the front or the fingers on the back. The thumb never shows through the object.
- When a character holds or offers food (e.g., bread): palm up, with the thumb and forefinger where they'd actually grip.
- Palm direction fits the gesture. A hand raised near the face has the palm toward the face, not flat to the camera.

**Arms, elbows, shoulders**
- Arms attach at the shoulder and are fully formed: upper arm, elbow, forearm, hand. Never detached, floating, or blob-shaped.
- No arm clipping into or "teleporting" through clothing. Angle the elbow slightly outward so the arm clearly sits outside the garment.
- Elbows bend naturally on both arms. Keep them low and relaxed unless the pose demands otherwise.
- No arms dangling in mid-air. Resting arms lie on the body or legs, and idle arms move slightly.

**Necks & heads**
- Necks are a realistic length, never elongated.
- The neck angles smoothly toward where the head is looking, with no Z-shaped zigzag.
- Add subtle V-shaped neck muscles on every character: the sternocleidomastoids, running from behind each ear down to the notch between the collarbones.
- The head sits centered on the neck and shoulders, not too high and not shifted sideways. Check this even when a beard hides the neck.
- Close-ups include the neck and the top of the clothing. Never a floating head.
- Face contours stay smooth and anatomically average, with no sharp inward dents in the cheeks.

**Faces & eyes**
- Characters in conversation look at the person they're addressing, on the correct side of the screen, not at the ground all the time.
- Speakers generally hold eye contact unless there's a reason not to. A brief glance down and back up signals recalling a memory or quote. Time visual glances to how the words are already functioning.
- One motivated look per beat, never the same glance twice in rapid succession unless there is an articulable reason. Contemplation or regret: one possible method is one slow look down with slight sadness on the face.
- Show emotions through the eyes. Awe mixed with fear could mean having a glance up at the source and back down with a fearful kind of face.
- Expressions fit the role. Check that neutral mouths don't read as smiles (e.g., guards should look tired, irritated, and reluctant).

**Hair & headwear**
- Hair is blended and lifelike with flowing strands. No stamped little circles, and no flat patches on top of the head that look like mud.
- Side locks attach to the scalp and lie against the head, never floating over the ears.
- Headwear sits low on the skull and follows its shape, never floating above it.
- Headwear has cloth detail (folds, wraps or a band, shading, highlights), not flat blobs.

**Legs, feet, poses**
- If the framing shows the lower body, every character has anatomically correct legs.
- Feet point where the body faces. A character facing the viewer has feet pointing forward.
- When a character sits, the legs agree with the torso. Never both legs over one side while the body faces the other way. An example pose is one leg down and the other folded up on a seat, with the hands resting on the raised leg or palm-down on the thighs.
- When a character kneels: knees on the ground, garment draped over the knees, feet hidden, and enough folds to read as kneeling.
- Clothing over legs shows the thighs and knees through folds and shading, never a solid blob.

**Clothing**
- Shoulder shadows are soft gradients that blend into the fabric color, with no hard brown lines.
- Cross-body drapes (cloth wrapping from one shoulder): the shoulder sticks out enough to hold the cloth up, and the arm emerges from under it. The cloth never looks like it's sliding off.
- Garment colors stay consistent from scene to scene.

**Proportions & design**
- Children have a shorter neck and a shorter torso and body.
- Characters must be clearly distinguishable, especially at the same age. Vary face shape, hair, skin tone, build, and clothing colors.
- If a character has a name label, show it the moment they first appear on screen, including as a younger or older version.

**Motion & continuity**
- No characters that are acting like an unmoving body statue. Every on-screen character keeps subtle idle motion: body sway, breathing, head and arm movement.
- During long dialogue, the speaker gestures and the listener keeps reacting. No long stretches of frozen bodies.
- Ease every head turn and gaze shift, with no instant jumps unless there is a specific reason.
- Avoid glitches when possible. Examples include 1–3 frame jolts, up-and-down bounces, or head "teleports."
- Emotional shaking is smooth and low-amplitude, paced like breathing or sobbing, not like a rapid jitter unless for a specific reason.
- Background figures already in the shot keep moving through the whole beat (e.g., walking off into the distance, getting smaller as they go).

**Tears**
- Each tear is one object that runs down and fades out as a whole, never segments vanishing one by one.
- Tears fade out naturally after falling.
- Tint tears slightly blue and translucent so they stand out against skin and white beards.
- Start tears partway into the beat (around halfway), not on its first frame.
- Use tears sparingly. Not every character or beat needs crying, and for quiet recognition or remorse, one tear from one eye reads best.

**Lighting & color**
- Lighting matches the visible light sources. A character with a light behind or near them is never near-black: give them rim light from that source plus enough fill to keep the face and body readable.
- Shadows are soft-edged and gradated, never muddy patches.
- Night and mood tints stay gentle. Lighting shades clothing but never turns it a different color (e.g., everything going dark blue).

**Staging, props, environment**
- Make every scripted action unmistakable through pose, props, and camera angle. Reading means both hands holding the scroll or letter with the eyes on it. A knock is shown clearly, from outside the door.
- In two-person exchanges, angle the characters (profile views when needed) so it's clear who's looking at whom.
- Objects rest on surfaces; add a simple table or ledge if needed. Avoid floating for objects that aren't supposed to be floating.
- When a door opens, animate a real door swinging on its hinges. No static stand-in images.
- Brick and stone look gritty, weathered, and lifelike.
